## 1. Opening · Kind of Blue · Miles Davis · 1959

> August 17, 1959. Columbia Studios, New York.
>
> Miles Davis walks in and hands six musicians a slip of paper each. A few scales on it. That's it. **"We just play it,"** he says.
>
> No rehearsal. What comes out is *Kind of Blue*.

---

## 2. Ragtime · Maple Leaf Rag · Scott Joplin · 1899

> Roll the clock back sixty years.
>
> Missouri, 1899. A 31-year-old mixed-race pianist sits down at a secondhand piano in a small town called Sedalia. His left hand keeps European time, dutifully. His right hand steps right between the beats.
>
> The two hands fight. The fight lasts three minutes. They call it *Maple Leaf Rag*.
>
> It isn't jazz. **But it fathered jazz.**

---

## 3. New Orleans · Hot Five & Hot Seven · Louis Armstrong · 1925–1928

> Everybody knows jazz started in New Orleans.
>
> But have you ever asked — **why there?**
>
> New Orleans, 1900. The French came. The Spanish came. The Americans came. The Caribbean came every day. In one city, **four languages running through the streets — French, Spanish, English, Creole.**
>
> The city had one more thing — **the only place in America where Black kids were allowed to learn music.**
>
> Those kids grew up reading European scores by day, listening to ragtime by night, hearing Caribbean drums in the street. They picked up trumpets, trombones, clarinets, pianos, drums — walked into the bars, **no sheet music, no conductor, a handful of people playing together** — a song going wherever their heads took them.
>
> That, later, is what people called jazz.
>
> One of those kids, born 1901, was named Louis Armstrong. He walked into a studio in 1925 and told the band:
>
> **"Let me play alone for two minutes. Wait for me."**
>
> Every jazz solo since starts the clock from there.

---

## 4. Swing · Sing, Sing, Sing · Benny Goodman · 1937

> Every weekend, dance halls across America were packed.
>
> Jazz wasn't a five-piece New Orleans bar band anymore. It walked into the big rooms in New York and Chicago, **and the bands grew to fifteen, twenty people.** They called it big band.
>
> To make a thousand-person hall hear them clearly, these guys wrote the music tight. But they kept one move: **a solo slot or two in the middle of every song, where the band's best stepped out.**
>
> And they did one more thing — **they taught music how to walk. One heavy step, one light step.**
>
> Two notes, originally the same length — stretch the first, squeeze the second. The whole song starts to sway.
>
> They had a name for it back then — **swing.**
>
> Next up: recorded 1937, eight minutes long. The opening drum break — **even someone who's never heard jazz will recognize it.**

## 5. Bebop · Ko-Ko · Charlie Parker · 1945

> 1945. 52nd Street, New York. A little club called the Three Deuces.
>
> Charlie Parker walks up. Doesn't say a word. Plays the first note.
>
> The dancers stop on the floor. **They have no idea what this is.**
>
> The track is called *Ko-Ko*. Better sit down for this one.

---

## 6. Cool / Hard Bop · Birth of the Cool / Moanin' · 1949 / 1958

> Bebop ran too hot. So hot it burned itself out.
>
> Late 40s. Miles Davis takes bebop and puts it in a chamber room. **Slower. Softer. The music starts breathing again.** The crew moves out to California and pushes the sound inward — restrained, calm, cool. That's cool jazz. The cooled-down bebop.
>
> But cool got too cold.
>
> 1954. Drummer Art Blakey and pianist Horace Silver start a band in New York called the Jazz Messengers. **"Jazz can't lose its soul,"** they say. They pull together bebop's mind, cool's polish, **and the gospel and blues that started everything.** That's hard bop.
>
> Two records coming up. One cool, one hard, almost ten years apart.
>
> **In those ten years, jazz first locked itself in a chamber — then walked back out into the world.**

---

## 7. Modal Jazz · So What / A Love Supreme · 1959 / 1965

> The next two records ask you to sit down.
>
> 1959. Miles Davis again. Columbia Studios again. But this time he's tearing down the last wall bebop left standing.
>
> Bebop players ran through thirty chords at full speed. Miles asks: **why not give them two scales and let them walk slow?**
>
> All he hands the band is two modes — **D Dorian and E♭ Dorian.** What's Dorian? **A scale that sounds a little brighter than minor, a little mysterious.** *So What*, nine minutes, uses only those two. Nothing else.
>
> Fewer chords. More air. **Slow. Spacious. Let the players speak for themselves — that's modal jazz.**
>
> And one man went further than Miles. **John Coltrane.**
>
> He was in Miles's band, but they fired him for heroin. **Lucky break** — he went home, kicked the habit, went through what he called a "spiritual awakening." **Seven years later, 1964, he finished *A Love Supreme*.**
>
> That record sits between modal and the free jazz coming up next — **it's modal taking one step out the door.**

---

## 8. Free Jazz · The Shape of Jazz to Come / Ascension · 1959 / 1965

> This next man broke every rule.
>
> May 1959. California. Ornette Coleman walks into Atlantic Studios with three others — Don Cherry on pocket trumpet, Charlie Haden on bass, Billy Higgins on drums. **He himself plays a plastic saxophone.**
>
> No piano.
>
> What they record has **no chords, no fixed key, no fixed measures.** The players listen to each other and answer back. Everyone's equal — no leader, no backing. Ornette gave it a name: **harmolodic** — meaning **harmony, melody, rhythm all equal, no one in charge.**
>
> That's free jazz.
>
> The record's title is just as bold — ***The Shape of Jazz to Come.***
>
> **John Coltrane was still pushing. Six years later, he recorded *Ascension* — forty minutes straight through, no second take, pure improvisation.**
>
> But listen — **jazz walked sixty years. It started with a bunch of Black kids in the New Orleans streets, improvising together. It went the long way around. And it came back to improvising together.**

## 9. Jazz Fusion · Bitches Brew · Miles Davis · 1969

> August 1969. Columbia Studio B, New York. Miles Davis calls twelve musicians into the room — three keyboard players sitting at once, electric guitar, electric bass, two drum kits, a stack of percussion. He hands out no full charts. Just a tempo. A few chords. A note about the key. Then the tape rolls. Three days. They just play.
>
> When it's done, Teo Macero cuts the tape, splices, loops. March 1970, a double LP comes out. *Bitches Brew*.
>
> Miles was 43 that year. The year before, he'd dropped *In a Silent Way*. Ten years before that, *Kind of Blue*. He could have lived off those albums for the rest of his life. He didn't. He was listening to Hendrix. To Sly Stone. To James Brown. He heard things in those records he hadn't played yet.
>
> That's jazz fusion. A jazz trumpet flies up top. A funk backbeat holds the floor. Electric keys spread through the whole record like water.
>
> ——
>
> This is the shape of the Jazz river by 1970.
>
> A hundred years. One generation after another. Every few years someone — right after laying down their strongest card — chose not to play it again. They threw away what they already knew, to go touch what they didn't.
>
> Because for them, **doing what you already know is writing your own ending.** Repeating a market-proven album. Repeating a sound the crowd already loves. In their accounting, that isn't stability. **That's a stop. That's Game Over.**
>
> 1969. The 43-year-old, body already breaking down — when he carried that electric guitar into the studio, he wasn't chasing more listeners. He was refusing to write his own ending.
